# Horton (2017) — The Effects of Algorithmic Labor Market Recommendations

**Retrieved:** 2026-08-09. *Journal of Labor Economics*, Vol. 35, No. 2 (April 2017).
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1086/689213 · Working paper: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2346486
Submitted January 2014, accepted March 2016, published online 2017-01-26. 122 Crossref citations.

**Why it is in this library.** Published randomised experiments on *recruiter-to-candidate*
outreach essentially do not exist — the ethics of deceiving job seekers rules out the audit
design, and platforms rarely publish. This is the closest: a real labour-market platform
randomised which employers got recruiting recommendations, then measured whether reaching out
actually produced hires. It answers the question that sits *upstream* of every copy benchmark:
does proactive outreach create hiring, or just move it around?

---

## Abstract (verbatim)

> "Algorithmically recommending workers to employers for the purpose of recruiting can
> substantially increase hiring: in an experiment conducted in an online labor market, employers
> with technical job vacancies that received recruiting recommendations had a 20% higher fill
> rate compared to the control. There is no evidence that the treatment crowded out hiring of
> nonrecommended candidates. The experimentally induced recruits were highly positively selected
> and were statistically indistinguishable from the kinds of workers employers recruit 'on their
> own.' Recommendations were most effective for job openings that were likely to receive a
> smaller applicant pool."

---

## Findings

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Fill rate | **+20%** for employers with technical vacancies who received recruiting recommendations, vs control |
| Crowd-out | **None detected** — the treatment did not displace hiring of non-recommended candidates. Outreach created incremental hires rather than substituting for inbound. |
| Quality of induced recruits | *"highly positively selected and were statistically indistinguishable from the kinds of workers employers recruit 'on their own.'"* — algorithmic sourcing did not degrade candidate quality |
| Heterogeneity | *"Recommendations were most effective for job openings that were likely to receive a smaller applicant pool."* — outreach pays most where inbound is thinnest |

Definitional note from the paper: an employer's invitation to a worker is treated as recruiting
when it occurred within 1 hour of posting the job.

---

## Key Conclusions

- **Sourcing causes hires; it does not merely reallocate them.** This is the randomised version
  of the observational vendor claim (Gem: sourced candidates ~8x more likely to be hired;
  direct sourcing delivers 11% of hires from 2.6% of applications). Vendor data cannot rule out
  selection; this design can, and it survives.
- **The returns concentrate where the inbound pool is thin.** Which is the quantitative case for
  outbound on hard-to-fill and niche roles specifically, rather than as a uniform motion.
- **Targeting outperforms messaging as a lever.** The treatment here was *which candidates were
  surfaced*, not what was written to them, and it produced a 20% fill-rate effect — larger than
  any copy variable measured in any observational report in this library.
- **Caveat on transfer:** an online freelance marketplace has far lower friction than a
  full-time senior hire, and "fill rate" is not "reply rate". Use this for the direction and the
  causal claim, not for a benchmark number.
