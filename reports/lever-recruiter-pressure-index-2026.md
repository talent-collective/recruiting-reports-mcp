# Lever — 2026 Recruiter Pressure Index: A State-by-State Map of the U.S. Talent War

**Source:** https://www.lever.co/blog/2026-recruiter-pressure-index
**Published:** 2026-06-16
**Data:** All 50 U.S. states + Washington, D.C.; sourced from U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey) and LAUS (Local Area Unemployment Statistics)
**Period:** BLS data as of November–December 2025 (most recent available at time of publication)
**Best for:** Regional hiring difficulty planning, workforce supply-demand analysis by state, benchmarking local recruiting conditions against national norms

---

## Methodology

Lever constructed the Recruiter Pressure Index using official BLS data for all 51 jurisdictions. Three metrics were scored:

| Metric | Data Source | Weight |
|--------|------------|--------|
| Job Openings Rate | JOLTS, November 2025 | 40% |
| Quits Rate | JOLTS, December 2025 | 30% |
| Unemployment Rate (inverted) | LAUS, December 2025 | 30% |

Each metric was min-max normalized to a 0–100 scale across all 51 jurisdictions. The unemployment rate was inverted so that lower unemployment = higher pressure score. Final scores reflect the weighted composite.

## Top 5 Highest-Pressure States (Hardest to Hire)

| Rank | State | Score | Pressure Pattern |
|------|-------|-------|-----------------|
| 1 | South Dakota | 82.1 | Small labor pool, high openings, high quits |
| 2 | North Dakota | ~76 | Energy sector demand, thin talent base |
| 3 | Oklahoma | ~72 | High openings rate relative to workforce size |
| 4 | Vermont | ~70 | Low unemployment + elevated quits |
| 5 | Alaska | ~68 | Remote geography, seasonal demand volatility |

**Common pattern for high-pressure states:** Small overall labor pools, elevated job openings rates relative to workforce size, and high voluntary quit rates—indicating active candidate mobility and low supply of replacement workers.

| Rank | State | Score | Pressure Pattern |
|------|-------|-------|-----------------|
| 51 (last) | Washington, D.C. | 8.3 | Highest unemployment (6.7%), low openings |
| 50 | California | 17.5 | High unemployment offsets large job market |
| 49 | Washington State | ~22 | Tech layoff concentration, elevated supply |
| 48 | Nevada | ~24 | Hospitality rebalancing, elevated unemployment |
| 47 | Massachusetts | ~26 | Post-biotech/tech cooldown, candidate surplus |

**Common pattern for low-pressure states:** High unemployment rates that inflate candidate supply — even in states with large, active economies. D.C.'s 6.7% unemployment rate (the highest in the country) creates a uniquely employer-favorable market despite being a high-cost-of-living hub.

---

## Regional Patterns

- **Great Plains and Mountain West** dominate the high-pressure list: small state economies with persistent labor shortages and mobile workforces
- **West Coast and major tech hubs** cluster at the low end: post-2022–2024 tech sector contractions have flooded these markets with available candidates
- **Sun Belt** is mixed: states like Oklahoma show high pressure while Nevada and Florida markets have softened
- **Northeast** varies significantly: Vermont at the top, Massachusetts near the bottom — small-state dynamics drive Vermont's tight labor supply

---

## Key Conclusions

- Geographic hiring conditions vary dramatically: South Dakota (82.1) is nearly 10x harder to recruit in than Washington D.C. (8.3) on this index — the same role in two different states is a fundamentally different recruiting challenge
- Major tech-hub states (California, Washington, Massachusetts) are currently among the easiest markets to hire in, driven by elevated unemployment and tech-sector candidate surplus from 2022–2025 layoffs
- Small Great Plains and Mountain West states face structural labor shortages regardless of broader economic conditions — recruiters in these markets need to invest in employer brand, relocation packages, and remote-work options
- High quit rates in the top-pressure states signal that even successfully hired candidates are more likely to leave — retention strategies matter as much as recruiting tactics in these markets
- Lever recommends TA teams use regional pressure scores to set realistic hiring timelines and adjust sourcing budgets by location — a single national benchmark understates difficulty in high-pressure states and overstates it in low-pressure ones
