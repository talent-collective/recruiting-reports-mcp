# Milkman, Akinola & Chugh (2015) — "What Happens Before?"

**Retrieved:** 2026-08-09. Manuscript: https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~dchugh/articles/SSRN22Apr.pdf ·
APA release: https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/apl-0000022.pdf ·
SSRN: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2063742
Published in the *Journal of Applied Psychology*, Online First 2015-04-13
(supplemental: http://dx.doi.org/10.1037/apl0000022.supp).

**Why it is in this library.** It is a **randomised cold-email experiment at scale** where the
message text was held identical and only the *sender's apparent identity* varied. It is the
cleanest published demonstration that who a cold email appears to come from moves reply rates
independently of what it says — the finding that most constrains how much copy optimisation can
ever be worth. It is not about recruiting outreach; it is about a stranger emailing a busy
professional to ask for time, which is structurally the same act.

---

## Abstract (verbatim, published version)

> "Little is known about how discrimination manifests before individuals formally apply to
> organizations or how it varies within and between organizations. We address this knowledge gap
> through an audit study in academia of over 6,500 professors at top U.S. universities drawn
> from 89 disciplines and 259 institutions. In our experiment, professors were contacted by
> fictional prospective students seeking to discuss research opportunities prior to applying to
> a doctoral program. Names of students were randomly assigned to signal gender and race (White,
> Black, Hispanic, Indian, Chinese), but messages were otherwise identical. We hypothesized that
> discrimination would appear at the informal 'pathway' preceding entry to academia and would
> vary by discipline and university as a function of faculty representation and pay. We found
> that when considering requests from prospective students seeking mentoring in the future,
> faculty were significantly more responsive to White males than to all other categories of
> students, collectively, particularly in higher-paying disciplines and private institutions.
> Counterintuitively, the representation of women and minorities and discrimination were
> uncorrelated, a finding that suggests greater representation cannot be assumed to reduce
> discrimination. This research highlights the importance of studying decisions made before
> formal entry points into organizations and reveals that discrimination is not evenly
> distributed within and between organizations."

## Design (verbatim from the manuscript)

> "we present new analyses of a field experiment in which 6,548 tenure-track professors at 259
> top U.S. universities in 109 different PhD-granting disciplines were contacted by fictional
> prospective doctoral students seeking a meeting to discuss research opportunities along the
> pathway to graduate school. The names of the 'students' were randomly assigned to signal
> gender and race (White, Black, Hispanic, Indian, Chinese), but their messages were otherwise
> identical. Our outcome of interest is whether faculty responded to these inquiries; in
> particular, we zoom in on inquiries sent about future (rather than same-day) interactions,
> which have been shown to give rise to bias."

**Note on a discrepancy in the source itself:** the published abstract says *"89 disciplines and
259 institutions"* while the body says *"109 different PhD-granting disciplines"* and *"6,548
tenure-track professors at 259 top U.S. universities."* Secondary coverage also reports "258
top-ranked U.S. universities" and "6,300 different Ph.D. granting departments." Quote the body
figures and note the abstract's differ.

Faculty sample composition (as reported in secondary coverage of the paper): of 4,375 professors
selected at random — 87% Caucasian, 2% Hispanic, 1% Black, 3% Indian, 4% Chinese, 3% Other; 68% Male.

**Timing manipulation is load-bearing.** The bias appears on requests to meet *in the future*,
not same-day. An earlier related paper by the same authors (Milkman, Akinola & Chugh, 2012)
established that the future-request framing is where bias emerges.

## Results

- **Overall response rate: 67%** of emails sent to faculty received responses.
- Faculty were **significantly more responsive to White males** than to all other categories of
  students collectively.
- Bias was **strongest in higher-paying disciplines and at private institutions**.
- **Business** (most discriminatory field): women and minorities were collectively ignored at
  **2.2x** the rate of Caucasian males.
- **Humanities** (least discriminatory): still ignored at **1.4x** the rate of Caucasian males.
- **Representation and bias were uncorrelated** — more women/minorities in a discipline did not
  predict less discrimination against them.

## Design context the paper cites (useful audit-study benchmarks)

- White job candidates receive a **50% higher callback rate** for interviews than identical
  Black job candidates (Bertrand & Mullainathan, 2004).
- Black and Latino job applicants with clean records are treated like Whites just released from
  prison (Pager, Western & Bonikowski, 2009).

---

## Why audit design, in the authors' words

> "studies of discrimination in which individuals realize they are being observed (e.g.,
> qualitative and laboratory studies) may suffer from social desirability bias and thus fail to
> measure implicit, unconscious, or unintentional bias… To the extent that unconscious bias may
> be contributing to discrimination, unobtrusive methods for studying discrimination are critical."

---

## Key Conclusions for outreach practice

- **At fixed content, identity moved reply rates by up to 2.2x.** No subject-line or
  body-length optimisation in any vendor benchmark in this library reports an effect that large.
  Who the message is from, and who it is to, dominates how it is written.
- The **sender-identity effect is symmetric evidence** with Cochrane's male-signature finding
  (OR 0.55). Two independent randomised sources say the signature line is a variable.
- **A 67% baseline response rate** shows what a cold email can achieve when the ask is small,
  the sender is plausibly relevant, and the recipient's role includes answering such requests —
  the opposite end of the range from the ~5% recruiting cold email baseline.
- **Ethical limit:** this is a deception study run under academic review. The design is not
  replicable in commercial recruiting outreach. Its value here is the finding, not the method.
